\begin{abstract}
An action's consequences often arrive in two stages: an intermediate signal at once, and the
outcome that matters only after a delay. A recommender sees a click in seconds but a purchase in
days. With $K$ actions and delay $d$, the rate available when the state-to-outcome map is
unrestricted is $\Ot(\sqrt{(K+d)T})$ \citep{esposito2023}, so costs grow with the catalogue even
when many items behave alike. When can the intermediate signal remove that dependence? If the
outcome mean depends on the action only through the state it produced, one delayed outcome
informs every action that could have produced it. What then sets the cost is not how many actions
there are but how differently they behave, an \emph{effective dimension} $v_t\in[1,|\Sset|]$.
Running $d+1$ copies of exponential weights in rotation attains $\Ot(\sqrt{(d+1)V\log K})$ for any
fixed $V\ge\sum_tv_t$ chosen before the run, which beats the general rate when actions overlap.
Grouping similar states lowers the cost further, at a bias we make explicit. Sharing cannot remove the
cost of delay itself: a learner handed the exact losses from $d$ rounds ago still suffers
$\Omega(\sqrt{d\mathcal E\min\{1+\log q,T/d\}})$, where $\mathcal E$ bounds how far the losses
drift while it waits.
Intermediate feedback therefore helps only when the signal is both shared across actions and still
current, two independent requirements. Synthetic experiments on the practical
single-copy variant, not covered by the theorem, cut regret by up to $79$ per cent.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Delayed outcomes are common in sequential decision-making. A system must choose an action now
even though the outcome it cares about may arrive much later. Recommendation is a representative
example. An item is shown immediately, an engagement signal such as a click or browsing depth is
observed soon after, and conversion or retention may only be known days or weeks later. The
central question here is when such an intermediate signal can reduce the cost of learning before
the final outcome arrives.

In this interpretation, actions are catalogue items, states are intermediate engagement outcomes,
and the delayed outcome is conversion. Different items may induce similar distributions over
engagement states, so an outcome observed after one item can carry information about many others.
The
difficulty scales not with the catalogue size $K$ but with how differently the actions distribute
users across the intermediate states. Operationally, a catalogue with thousands of items can still
be easy to learn from if those items funnel users through only a few genuinely different
engagement patterns.

Bandits with intermediate observations formalize this. \citet{esposito2023} show that the \emph{state-to-loss} map decides the complexity, an
unrestricted one giving only the rate available with no intermediate observations; we fix that map and ask how much the \emph{action-to-state} structure helps.

Intermediate observations face two independent limitations: whether information can be shared
\emph{across actions}, and whether it stays useful \emph{across time}.

Across actions: because similar actions reach similar states, we can \emph{pool through the state},
charging a delayed outcome to every action that could have
produced the state observed. The resulting statistical cost is governed by an \emph{effective
dimension} $v_t$, which measures how distinguishable the action-induced state distributions are
under the learner's current play. When many actions behave similarly through the state channel,
$v_t$ can be far smaller than $K$.

Across time: delay creates a second difficulty even when the learner is handed an exact
description of the past. Let $m_t=c_{t-d}$ be the action-loss vector from $d$ rounds earlier. Its usefulness depends
on how far the losses have moved, which we measure by $\etwo=\sum_t\|c_t-m_t\|_\infty^2$
(Figure~\ref{fig:one}). Our lower bound asks
how much regret this mismatch forces even when $m_t$ is supplied for free.

\section{Related work}

Our results connect two largely separate lines of work: delayed feedback, and information sharing
across actions. \emph{Delayed bandits.} \citet{vernade2020} vary the action-to-state map while fixing the
state-to-loss map, whereas we fix the former and let the latter vary obliviously.
\citet{zimmert2020} give the optimal
$\Ot(\sqrt{KT}+\sqrt{D\log K})$ for total delay $D$, and \citet{esposito2023} allow an unknown and possibly adversarial
action-to-state map; we fix that channel instead, which is what makes its overlap geometry
measurable through $v_t$.
\emph{Stale predictions.} \citet{baron2025} derive upper bounds adapting to the inaccuracy of
evolving observations; our lower bound instead asks what delay-dependent cost is unavoidable in
stale-prediction error. \citet{wang2026} bound squared gradient prediction error without delay, so the delay factor and
its saturation are new here; \citet{zhang2026impatient} share the motivation in a stochastic
Bayesian model whereas ours is adversarial.
\emph{Mediator feedback} provides the closest structural connection: $v_t-1$ is the $\chi^2$
mutual information of \citet{eldowa2024}. Our contribution is not that quantity but its role when
the outcome attached to the observed state is delayed, and the separation of that structural
benefit from an independent temporal obstruction (Appendix~\ref{app:transfer}). Feedback graphs and off-policy coverage
\citep{gabbianelli2023} provide related forms of information sharing;
Appendix~\ref{app:related} gives the detailed comparison.

\section{Problem formulation}\label{sec:setting}

There are $T$ rounds, $K$ actions, a finite state space $\Sset$, and a fixed known delay $d$; all
logarithms are natural. Every round follows the chain
$A_t\xrightarrow{\;P\;}S_t\xrightarrow{\;\theta_t\;}X_t$: the learner draws $A_t$ from $x_t$,
observes $S_t\sim P(\cdot\mid A_t)$ at once, and receives $X_t\in[0,1]$ only at time $t+d$, with
$\Ex[X_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1},A_t,S_t]=\theta_t(S_t)$ and outcomes conditionally independent across
rounds given the states. The conditional mean depends on $A_t$ only through $S_t$. This
\emph{state-sufficiency} assumption is exactly what licenses cross-action pooling: after observing
an outcome at state $S_t$, the learner may use it to update every action that could have produced
that state. If an action has a direct effect on the outcome not represented in $S_t$, the argument
no longer applies. Here $x_t$ may depend only on $\mathcal F_{t-1}$, everything seen before $A_t$ is chosen; $X_r$ may first affect the action distribution
at round $r+d+1$, equivalently the round-$t$ decision uses realized outcomes only from rounds
$r\le t-d-1$.

The \emph{action-to-state matrix} $P\in[0,1]^{K\times|\Sset|}$ is row-stochastic and fixed, and the \emph{state-loss means} $\theta_t\in[0,1]^{|\Sset|}$ are \emph{oblivious}:
chosen in advance, not reacting to the learner. Action losses are $c_t=P\theta_t$ and performance
is the static \emph{pseudo-regret}, the gap to the best single action in hindsight,
$\Ex[R_T]=\Ex[\sum_tc_t(A_t)]-\min_a\sum_tc_t(a)$. \textbf{Oracle for the lower bound.} For Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} only, the learner also receives the \emph{stale action-loss vector} $m_t\triangleq c_{t-d}$ before choosing $A_t$, with $m_t=c_1$ for $t\le d$. This is not a
function of the observables, so a lower bound surviving it is stronger, and
\textsc{State-EXP3} never uses it. We measure how misleading $m_t$ is by $\etwo=\sum_t\|c_t-m_t\|_\infty^2\le T$, which stays small
when errors are small even if frequent. The geometry of $P$ thus governs sharing across actions
and the evolution of $\theta_t$ governs staleness across time, the two axes of
Section~\ref{sec:alg} and Section~\ref{sec:lower}. 

\section{Pooling through the state}\label{sec:alg}

When an outcome arrives, do not update only the action played: update every action by how likely
it was to produce the observed state. Here $P$ is known and the learner does not
receive $m_t$. Let $q_t(s)=\sum_ax_t(a)P(s\mid a)$ be the chance of seeing state $s$ this round,
and give each action $\hat c_t(a)=P(S_t\mid a)X_t/q_t(S_t)$. It is correct on average, and
how noisy it is depends on how much the actions overlap, through the \emph{effective dimension}
$v_t=\sum_sq_t(s)^{-1}\sum_ax_t(a)P(s\mid a)^2\in[1,|\Sset|]$.
\textsc{State-EXP3} runs $m$ copies of \textsc{EXP3} \citep{auer2002} in rotation, copy $i$ taking
every $m$th round, and adds $\hat c_t$ to a copy's running total once the outcome is usable. A
round costs $O(K)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:pool}, with pseudocode and the $\chi^2$ reading of $v_t$).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:sexp3}
Let $P$ be known and $(\theta_t)$ oblivious. Pick before the run any fixed number $V$ with
$\sum_tv_t\le V$ always. Then \textup{\textsc{State-EXP3}} with $m=d+1$ and the matching learning
rate satisfies
\[
\Ex[R_T]\le\sqrt{2(d+1)V\log K}.
\]
Writing $\bar v$ for the largest $v_t$ over all ways of playing, $V=\bar vT\le|\Sset|T$ is one such
choice, and it depends on $P$ alone.
\end{theorem}

So $K$, the number of actions, has been replaced by $V$, which counts how different the actions
really are. It falls to one when they all lead to the same states and rises when the state reveals
which action was played. The proof uses two facts: the shared estimate is correct on average and no noisier than $v_t$,
and with $d+1$ copies in rotation each copy has its own previous outcome before it acts again. With $V=\bar vT$ this beats the general rate $\Ot(\sqrt{(K+d)T})$
\citep{esposito2023} whenever $\bar v(d+1)\log K\lesssim K+d$.

The bound has three known gaps. Rotating copies multiplies the delay cost by $d+1$. It uses $v_t$ where $v_t-1$ would be correct, so it stays at $\sqrt{2(d+1)T\log K}$ even when every action
behaves the same way and the true regret is zero. It also ignores $\etwo$. 

\section{The representation trade-off}\label{sec:rep}

Some states may not be worth telling apart. Group them with a map $g:\Sset\to\mathcal Z$ and run
the same algorithm on $g(S_t)$, where $P^g(z\mid a)=\sum_{s:g(s)=z}P(s\mid a)$. Grouping never
raises the dimension, but the estimate now tracks a group average, so the widest spread of losses
inside a group, $\delta_t(g)=\max_z\max_{s,s':g(s)=g(s')=z}|\theta_t(s)-\theta_t(s')|$, is the
error introduced.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:rep}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3}, running on $g$ with $\sum_tv_t(g)\le V(g)$ gives
$\Ex[R_T]\le\sqrt{2(d+1)V(g)\log K}+2\sum_t\delta_t(g)$.
\end{theorem}

The two terms are the gain and the cost. Merging states need not preserve state-sufficiency, so
the grouped estimate is no longer right for $c_t$; it is right for a stand-in loss differing from
$c_t$ by at most $\delta_t(g)$ on every action (Appendix~\ref{app:etc}). Keep states apart and you preserve differences that matter but learn more
slowly; merge them and you share more but pay $2\sum_t\delta_t(g)$. The best grouping is the true one: with $16$ states built from
four hidden groups, groupings of size $1,2,4,8,16$ give regret $1590$, $831$, $754$, $920$,
$1080$, lowest at four, which no algorithm was told. The map $g$ is fixed in advance; learning it from data remains open.

\section{The cost of drift}\label{sec:lower}

We now ask how much drift costs even when the stale action losses are free.
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} shows a cost from time alone, not a joint overlap-drift bound: the
construction gives the $q$ drifting actions private states, removing useful pooling and forcing
$q\le|\Sset|-1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:lower}
Fix $T$, a delay $1\le d\le T/2$, a drift budget $\mathcal E\le T/4$, and
$1\le q\le\min\{K-1,|\Sset|-1\}$. Every algorithm, even one handed the exact losses from $d$ rounds
ago, meets some instance fixed in advance with $\etwo\le\mathcal E$ on which
$\Ex[R_T]=\Omega(\sqrt{d\,\mathcal E\min\{1+\log q,\,T/d\}})$. Without that help, and if $K\le T$,
the best regret any algorithm can guarantee is
$\Omega(\sqrt{KT}+\sqrt{d\,\mathcal E\min\{1+\log q,\,T/d\}})$, up to constants.
\end{theorem}

The first term, $\sqrt{KT}$, is the usual cost of exploring. The second is the cost of delay: it grows
with the wait, how far the losses move during it, and the number $q$ of directions the best fixed
action can use. The two come from different hard instances, so adding them
overstates the truth by at most a factor of two.

\section{What the experiments show}\label{sec:num}

Pooling gives large gains when many actions share a small number of state patterns.
A synthetic recommendation funnel
illustrates the low-dimension regime. With
$K=200$ catalogue items and six engagement states the estimated effective dimension is only $1.97$,
and the state-pooled variant cuts regret against action-level weighting by $79.3$, $63.6$ and
$38.9$ per cent at $d=10,50,200$. The funnel is hand-structured, not fitted to data, so it illustrates the mechanism rather than a
deployed system. On a Dirichlet family the gain grows with the catalogue, and an online plug-in $\hat P_t$ stays
within $0.5$ per cent of known $P$ (study~1: $|\Sset|=8$, $T=5000$, $d=20$, $100$ paired seeds).

delayed-bandit method the state channel still helps, cutting regret by $32$ to $68$ per cent on
the funnel (Appendix~\ref{app:num}).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:conj}

The results suggest a simple diagnostic. Pooling helps most when many actions lead to similar states, so the effective dimension falls well below the action count; coarsening strengthens
it when grouped states have similar outcome means; and fast-changing state-to-outcome relationships
limit how far delayed information can be trusted. 

\begin{conjecture}[open]\label{conj:add}
\textup{\textsc{State-EXP3}} with $m=1$ satisfies the first displayed bound, and some algorithm
achieves the second. Both statements remain unproved.
\[
\Ex[R_T]=\Ot\bigl(\sqrt{V\log K}+\sqrt{dT\log K}\bigr),
\qquad
\Ex[R_T]=\Ot\bigl(\sqrt{KT}+\sqrt{d\,\etwo\log K}\bigr).
\]
\end{conjecture}

Intermediate feedback is useful when it creates genuine sharing and stays predictive long enough
to act on; adapting automatically to both remains the main open problem
(Appendices~\ref{app:counter}, \ref{app:additive}).

\section{Extended related work}\label{app:related}

\citet{guinet2022} use the name \emph{effective dimension} for an unrelated quantity, the number of
directions a censored-feedback problem can resolve; it is not $v_t$ and the observation model
differs.

The minimax characterization of delayed adversarial bandits traces to \citet{cesabianchi2019}, and
\citet{joulani2013} give the additive form of the delay penalty in the stochastic case. On acting
before the loss is known, \citet{flaspohler2021} study optimism under delay directly,
\citet{wei2020} delimit when prediction error controls regret, \citet{hsieh2022} show that an
optimistic step must run at a larger scale than the update, and \citet{zhang2026} gives
second-order path-length bounds.

Structure that lets one observation inform several actions is the other half of the picture.
Feedback graphs \citep{alon2015} fix which actions are revealed together, and their stochastic
version \citep{esposito2022} randomizes that set. Factored rewards with intermediate observations
\citep{mussi2024} decompose the reward instead. Off-policy coverage \citep{gabbianelli2023} is the
closest analogue of our effective dimension, since its mismatch coefficient measures the same
kind of overlap between the distribution that generated the data and the one being evaluated. The
distinction from all of these is that a feedback graph randomizes \emph{who} is observed, whereas
our sensor randomizes \emph{what} is observed and the sharing is a consequence of $P$ rather than
a modeling primitive.

\section{The state-pooled estimator}\label{app:pool}

\paragraph{The algorithm.} Inputs are the action-to-state matrix $P$, the delay $d$, the number
of copies $m$, and the rate $\eta>0$. The analyzed setting is $m=d+1$ and the practical variant
is $m=1$. Round $r$'s outcome becomes usable at round $r+d+1$, which for $m=d+1$ is the next
round belonging to the copy that played round $r$.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt \parskip0pt \topsep3pt
\item Set $L_i\leftarrow(0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb R^K$ for $i=0,\dots,m-1$, and let $\mathcal P$ be
an empty table of rounds awaiting their outcome.
\item For $t=1,\dots,T$:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt \parskip0pt \topsep1pt
\item \emph{Deliver.} If $r\triangleq t-d-1\ge1$, read $(i_r,q_r(S_r),S_r,X_r)$ from
$\mathcal P$, remove it, and update only that copy,
$L_{i_r}(a)\leftarrow L_{i_r}(a)+P(S_r\mid a)X_r/q_r(S_r)$ for every $a$. This is the pooled
step, and it touches every action rather than the one played.
\item \emph{Select the copy.} $i\leftarrow t\bmod m$.
\item \emph{Play.} $x_t(a)\propto\exp(-\eta L_i(a))$, draw $A_t\sim x_t$, and observe $S_t$
at once.
\item \emph{Record.} Compute the scalar $q_t(S_t)=\sum_ax_t(a)P(S_t\mid a)$ and store
$(i,q_t(S_t),S_t,\cdot)$ in $\mathcal P$, to be completed when $X_t$ arrives at time $t+d$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

Steps (a), (c) and (d) are each $O(K)$ and there is no optimization subproblem, so a round costs
$O(K)$ once $P$ is stored. The state is held in the $m$ vectors $L_i$, which is $mK$ numbers, the
matrix $P$, which is $K|\Sset|$ numbers, and four scalars for each of the at most $d$ rounds in
flight. Only the scalar $q_r(S_r)$ is needed from round $r$, not the vector $x_r$.

\begin{remark}[The $\chi^2$ form]\label{rem:chi2}
Under $x_t$ the pair $(A_t,S_t)$ has law $x_t(a)P(s\mid a)$ with marginals $x_t$ and $q_t$, so
\begin{align*}
\chi^2\bigl(P_{A_t,S_t}\,\big\|\,P_{A_t}\!\otimes\!P_{S_t}\bigr)
&=\sum_{a,s}\frac{\bigl(x_t(a)P(s\mid a)-x_t(a)q_t(s)\bigr)^2}{x_t(a)q_t(s)}\\
&=\sum_{a,s}\frac{x_t(a)P(s\mid a)^2}{q_t(s)}-2+1\ =\ v_t-1,
\end{align*}
the cross term summing to $\sum_{a,s}x_t(a)P(s\mid a)=1$ and the last to
$\sum_{a,s}x_t(a)q_t(s)=1$. So $v_t=1$ exactly when $A_t$ and $S_t$ are independent, which pins the rows of $P$ only on the
support of $x_t$ and therefore means all rows agree whenever $x_t$ has full support, as
exponential weights does. If $P$ is deterministic and every state is reached by some action in
the support of $x_t$, then $v_t$ equals the number of states that support reaches. In particular,
under full-support play and an onto deterministic $P$, $v_t=|\Sset|$. This is an identity, not a bound, and it is used here only for reading
$v_t$, never in a proof.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:pool}
Fix $t$, let $x_t$ be the play distribution, $q_t(s)=\sum_ax_t(a)P(s\mid a)$ the induced state
distribution, and $\hat c_t(a)=P(S_t\mid a)X_t/q_t(S_t)$, which is well defined because only
states with $q_t(s)>0$ occur. For statements involving $1/x_t(a)$, restrict to actions with
$x_t(a)>0$; \textup{\textsc{State-EXP3}} itself has full support on every action. Then $\hat c_t(a)\ge0$, $\hat c_t(a)\le1/x_t(a)$,
$\Ex[\hat c_t(a)\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=c_t(a)$, and
\[
\sum_ax_t(a)\Ex[\hat c_t(a)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]\ \le\ v_t
:=\sum_{s:q_t(s)>0}\frac{\sum_ax_t(a)P(s\mid a)^2}{q_t(s)},\qquad 1\le v_t\le|\Sset| ,
\]
states of zero probability being omitted throughout, which is the convention $0/0=0$.
The action-level $\tilde c_t(a)=\mathbf 1\{A_t=a\}X_t/x_t(a)$ has the same first three properties
and weighted second moment at most $K$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Nonnegativity is immediate from $X_t\ge0$. Given $\mathcal F_{t-1}$ the state $S_t$ has
distribution $q_t$, and $\Ex[X_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1},S_t=s]=\theta_t(s)$ by the model, since that
conditional mean does not depend on the action, so
$\Ex[\hat c_t(a)\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=\sum_sq_t(s)P(s\mid a)\theta_t(s)/q_t(s)=c_t(a)$. For the
range, $q_t(s)\ge x_t(a)P(s\mid a)$ for every $a$, so $P(s\mid a)/q_t(s)\le1/x_t(a)$ and
$X_t\le1$. For the second moment, $X_t^2\le1$ gives
$\Ex[\hat c_t(a)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]\le\sum_sP(s\mid a)^2/q_t(s)$, and exchanging the sums
gives $v_t$. Each summand of $v_t$ is at most $1$, since $P(s\mid a)\le1$ makes
$\sum_ax_t(a)P(s\mid a)^2\le q_t(s)$, and at least $q_t(s)$, since Jensen applied to
$a\mapsto P(s\mid a)$ under $x_t$ gives $\sum_ax_t(a)P(s\mid a)^2\ge q_t(s)^2$. Summing the two
bounds gives $1\le v_t\le|\Sset|$. The action-level claims are the same computation with the
state replaced by the played action, whose weighted second moment is $\sum_ax_t(a)/x_t(a)=K$.
\end{proof}

The two ends are attained. If every action induces the same state distribution then
$v_t=\sum_sq_t(s)=1$, and if $P$ is deterministic, each action reaching a single state, then
every $P(s\mid a)$ is $0$ or $1$ and each summand is exactly $1$, so $v_t$ counts the states
reached and equals $|\Sset|$ when every state has an action reaching it. Disjoint row supports
give $v_t=K$ rather than $|\Sset|$, since the summands over one action's private states add to
$\sum_sP(s\mid a)=1$, so a sensor with many private states per action resolves few of them. So $v_t$ reads the
overlap between the rows of $P$ rather than their length, and $\bar v=\sup_xv(x)$, the supremum
over play distributions, depends on $P$ alone. An outcome observed at a state updates the estimate
for every action that can reach it, which is why the construction of Section~\ref{sec:lower} gives
each drifting coordinate a private state, forcing $v_t=|\Sset|$ there. The immediately observed
pairs $(A_t,S_t)$ identify $P$ separately, though Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3} assumes it known.

\section{Proofs of Theorems~\ref{thm:sexp3} and \ref{thm:rep}}\label{app:etc}

\textbf{Proof idea.} The pooled estimator is unbiased for every action, while
Proposition~\ref{prop:pool} bounds its play-weighted second moment by $v_t$ rather than $K$. With
$m=d+1$ interleaved copies, the outcome generated on a round belonging to one copy is available
before that copy acts again, so each copy is an ordinary exponential-weights process with
immediate feedback. Summing the $d+1$ potential inequalities gives $(d+1)\log K/\eta$, and the
pooled second moments give $\tfrac\eta2\sum_tv_t$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3}]
Fix a copy $i$ and let $T_i$ be the number of rounds it owns, so $\sum_iT_i=T$. Round $t$'s
outcome is usable from round $t+d+1$, which with $m=d+1$ is exactly $t+m$, the copy's next round,
so within a copy the feedback is undelayed and $L_i$ carries exactly the estimates of that copy's
earlier rounds. Exponential weights on nonnegative losses give, for any action $a^\star$ and any
$\eta>0$,
\[
\sum_{t\in i}\bigl(\langle x_t,\hat c_t\rangle-\hat c_t(a^\star)\bigr)
\le\frac{\log K}{\eta}+\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{t\in i}\sum_ax_t(a)\hat c_t(a)^2 ,
\]
by the usual potential telescoping with $e^{-z}\le1-z+z^2/2$, which needs $z\ge0$ and therefore
$\hat c_t\ge0$, supplied by Proposition~\ref{prop:pool}. Each $x_t$ is
$\mathcal F_{t-1}$-measurable, because $L_i$ then holds only rounds $r\le t-m=t-d-1$, so taking
expectations turns the left side into $\sum_{t\in i}(\langle x_t,c_t\rangle-c_t(a^\star))$ by
unbiasedness and the quadratic term into at most $\Ex[\sum_{t\in i}v_t]$, again by
Proposition~\ref{prop:pool}. Summing over the $m=d+1$ copies and using
$\sum_i\min_a\sum_{t\in i}c_t(a)\le\min_a\sum_tc_t(a)$, which is what lets the copies be compared
against one common action, gives $\Ex[R_T]\le(d+1)\log K/\eta+\tfrac\eta2\Ex[\sum_tv_t]$. If
$\sum_tv_t\le V$ surely then $\eta=\sqrt{2(d+1)\log K/V}$ balances the two terms and gives
$\sqrt{2(d+1)V\log K}$, and $V=\bar vT$ is admissible by the definition of $\bar v$.
\end{proof}

The delay enters as a factor because the copies do not share their estimates, each paying
$\log K/\eta$ over $T/(d+1)$ rounds. Sharing them is exactly the $m=1$ algorithm of
Conjecture~\ref{conj:add}, whose analysis is open.

\begin{lemma}[Coarsening]\label{lem:coarse}
For every play distribution and every $g$, $v_t(g)\le v_t$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
It suffices to merge two states, the general case following by induction on the merges, and a
merged group of zero probability contributes nothing on either side by the convention above.
Write $P_1,P_2$ for the two columns, $A_j=\sum_ax_t(a)P_j(a)^2$, $B_j=\sum_ax_t(a)P_j(a)$ and
$\lambda=B_1/(B_1+B_2)$. By Cauchy--Schwarz,
$(P_1+P_2)^2\le\lambda^{-1}P_1^2+(1-\lambda)^{-1}P_2^2$ pointwise in $a$, so
$\sum_ax_t(a)(P_1+P_2)^2\le(B_1+B_2)(A_1/B_1+A_2/B_2)$, and dividing by the merged denominator
$B_1+B_2$ shows the merged summand is at most $A_1/B_1+A_2/B_2$, the two it replaces.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:rep}]
Running the algorithm on $g$ is running it on the sensor $(\mathcal Z,P^g)$. The surrogate
losses below depend on $x_t$ and so are predictable rather than oblivious, but the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3} uses obliviousness nowhere, only that each $c^g_t$ is
$\mathcal F_{t-1}$-measurable given the play, so it applies unchanged and bounds regret measured
in the surrogate losses
$c^g_t(a)=\sum_zP^g(z\mid a)\bar\theta_t(z)$, where
$\bar\theta_t(z)=\sum_{s:g(s)=z}q_t(s)\theta_t(s)/q_t^g(z)$ is the group mean the grouped
estimator is unbiased for, by the same computation as in Proposition~\ref{prop:pool} with
$S_t$ replaced by $g(S_t)$. Fix $a$ and $z$. Both $\{q_t(s)/q_t^g(z)\}$ and
$\{P(s\mid a)/P^g(z\mid a)\}$ are probability vectors on $\{s:g(s)=z\}$, so their
$\theta_t$-averages lie in a common interval of length $\delta_t(g)$ and differ by at most
$\delta_t(g)$. Weighting by $P^g(z\mid a)$ and summing over $z$ gives
$|c^g_t(a)-c_t(a)|\le\delta_t(g)$ for every $a$, hence
$\langle x_t,c_t\rangle-c_t(a^\star)\le\langle x_t,c^g_t\rangle-c^g_t(a^\star)+2\delta_t(g)$.
Summing over $t$ adds $2\sum_t\delta_t(g)$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:coarse} makes $V(g)$ no larger than
a bound valid for the raw states.
\end{proof}

\section{An unknown action-to-state map}\label{app:unknown}

\paragraph{The plug-in estimator.}
The pairs $(A_t,S_t)$ arrive without delay, so $P$ is estimable from the learner's own play while
the outcomes are still in flight. Let $N_a(t)$ count the rounds before $t$ that played $a$ and
$N_{a,s}(t)$ those that also produced $s$, and set
$\hat P_t(s\mid a)=(N_{a,s}(t)+\alpha)/(N_a(t)+\alpha|\Sset|)$ for a smoothing constant
$\alpha\in(0,1]$ fixed independently of $T$, which is row-stochastic with every entry positive, so
$\hat q_t=x_t^\top\hat P_t$ is positive too, and both are $\mathcal F_{t-1}$-measurable. Smoothing moves an entry
from the empirical row by at most $\alpha(|\Sset|-1)/(N_a(t)+\alpha|\Sset|)$, attained when every
observation of $a$ sits on one state, and moves the row in $\ell_1$ by at most twice that. Both
are of lower order than the sampling term at fixed $\alpha$ and must be carried otherwise, and
Appendix~\ref{app:num} shows the choice matters in practice. The plug-in estimate is
$\hat c_t(a)=\hat P_t(S_t\mid a)X_t/\hat q_t(S_t)$. Write
$\varepsilon_t=\max_a\|\hat P_t(\cdot\mid a)-P(\cdot\mid a)\|_1$ for its accuracy and
$\rho_t=\max_{s:q_t(s)>0}|\hat q_t(s)-q_t(s)|/q_t(s)$ for the relative error it induces on the
state distribution, states of zero probability under the play being omitted since the estimator
never charges them.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:plugin}
On $\{\rho_t\le\tfrac12\}$ the plug-in estimate satisfies $\hat c_t\ge0$ and
$\sum_ax_t(a)\Ex[\hat c_t(a)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]\le2\hat v_t\le2|\Sset|$, and its conditional
mean $\bar c_t$ satisfies $\sum_ax_t(a)(\bar c_t(a)-c_t(a))=0$ exactly, for every $\hat P_t$
and with no hypothesis at all, and $|\bar c_t(a)-c_t(a)|\le2\varepsilon_t(a)+2\rho_t$ for every $a$, where
$\varepsilon_t(a)=\|\hat P_t(\cdot\mid a)-P(\cdot\mid a)\|_1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\Delta_t=\hat P_t-P$ and $\delta_t=\hat q_t-q_t=\sum_ax_t(a)\Delta_t(\cdot\mid a)$.
Substituting these into $\bar c_t(a)=\sum_sq_t(s)\hat P_t(s\mid a)\theta_t(s)/\hat q_t(s)$ and
cancelling gives
$\bar c_t(a)-c_t(a)=\sum_s\theta_t(s)[q_t(s)\Delta_t(s\mid a)-P(s\mid a)\delta_t(s)]/\hat q_t(s)$,
so with $\theta_t\in[0,1]^{|\Sset|}$,
\[
|\bar c_t(a)-c_t(a)|\ \le\ \sum_s\frac{q_t(s)|\Delta_t(s\mid a)|+P(s\mid a)|\delta_t(s)|}{\hat q_t(s)} .
\]
The play-weighted identity needs no hypothesis. By definition
$\sum_ax_t(a)\hat P_t(s\mid a)=\hat q_t(s)$, so $\sum_ax_t(a)\hat c_t(a)=X_t$ whatever $\hat P_t$
is, and taking conditional expectations gives
$\sum_ax_t(a)\bar c_t(a)=\sum_sq_t(s)\theta_t(s)=\langle x_t,c_t\rangle$. On
$\{\rho_t\le\tfrac12\}$ we have $q_t\le2\hat q_t$, and for the per-action bias the first sum of
the display is at most $2\|\Delta_t(\cdot\mid a)\|_1=2\varepsilon_t(a)$ and the second at most
$2\sum_sP(s\mid a)|\delta_t(s)|/q_t(s)\le2\rho_t$, because $P(\cdot\mid a)$ is a probability
vector. The play-weighted identity does \emph{not} survive reweighting: at $x_t=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$,
$P=I$, $\hat P=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}.9&.1\\.2&.8\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and
$\theta_t=(0,1)$ the bias is $(\tfrac19,-\tfrac19)$ with zero $x_t$-average, while weights
$(1,0)$ leave $\tfrac1{18}$. For the second moment,
$\sum_ax_t(a)\Ex[\hat c_t(a)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]
\le\sum_s\frac{q_t(s)}{\hat q_t(s)}\cdot\frac{\sum_ax_t(a)\hat P_t(s\mid a)^2}{\hat q_t(s)}
\le2\hat v_t$, and $\hat v_t\le|\Sset|$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:pool} applied to $\hat P_t$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The warm-start guarantee.}
\textsc{Warm-start State-EXP3} plays uniformly for $n_0$ rounds, forms $\hat P=\hat P_{n_0+1}$
from those rounds alone, discards the cumulative estimates together with every outcome of a
warm-start round that has not yet arrived, and then runs
\textsc{State-EXP3} with $m=d+1$, that frozen $\hat P$, and each play mixed with the uniform
action at rate $\gamma$. Freezing makes $\hat P$ measurable with respect to the first phase, so
$\varepsilon$ and $\rho$ are fixed before the second phase begins, and discarding is what keeps
the first phase's inaccurate updates out of every later distribution.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:unknown}
Suppose $\bar p(s)\ge1/(\kappa|\Sset|)$ for every $s$, fix $0<\gamma\le\tfrac12$ and
$1\le d\le T/2$, and take
$n_0\ge c\,\gamma^{-2}K\kappa^2|\Sset|^2\log(K|\Sset|T)$ for a universal $c$. Then
\textup{\textsc{Warm-start State-EXP3}} satisfies, for every $\eta>0$,
\[
\Ex[R_T]\ \le\ n_0+1+\gamma T+\frac{(d+1)\log K}{\eta}+2\eta|\Sset|T+T\bigl(4\bar\varepsilon+4\bar\rho\bigr),
\]
where $\bar\varepsilon=\Ot(\sqrt{K|\Sset|/n_0})$ and
$\bar\rho=\Ot(\kappa|\Sset|\sqrt{K/n_0}/\gamma)\le\tfrac12$ bound $\varepsilon$ and $\rho$ on an
event of probability at least $1-1/T$. Tuning $\eta$ and then
$\gamma\asymp(\kappa|\Sset|)^{1/2}(K/n_0)^{1/4}$ and
$n_0\asymp T^{4/5}(\kappa|\Sset|)^{2/5}K^{1/5}$ gives
$\Ex[R_T]=\Ot\bigl(\sqrt{d\,|\Sset|T\log K}+(\kappa|\Sset|)^{2/5}K^{1/5}T^{4/5}\bigr)$, provided
$T=\widetilde\Omega(K(\kappa|\Sset|)^2)$. That condition constrains $T$ against $K$, $|\Sset|$ and
$\kappa$ only, so the hypothesis $d\le T/2$ is what carries the delay. Together they give
$n_0\le T/2\le T-d$, and the same condition supplies the lower bound above with some
$\gamma\le\tfrac12$. Outside that regime the tuning is vacuous and $R_T\le T$ is the
statement.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The first phase has losses in $[0,1]$, so it contributes at most $n_0$. Let $G$ be the event that
$N_a(n_0+1)\ge n_0/(2K)$ for every $a$, that
$\|\hat P(\cdot\mid a)-P(\cdot\mid a)\|_1\le\bar\varepsilon$ for every $a$, and that
$\max_{s,a}|\hat P(s\mid a)-P(s\mid a)|\le\bar\rho\gamma/(\kappa|\Sset|)$. Uniform play gives
$\Ex[N_a]=n_0/K$, so a multiplicative Chernoff bound, the $\ell_1$ deviation bound for a
multinomial, Hoeffding, and a union bound over the $K|\Sset|$ entries put
$\Pr(G^c)\le1/T$ at the stated $n_0$, the pseudo-counts adding
$2\alpha(|\Sset|-1)/(N_a+\alpha|\Sset|)$ to each $\ell_1$ deviation and half that to each entry,
of lower order at fixed $\alpha$. Off $G$ bound the second phase by $T$, contributing at most $1$.

On $G$ the quantity $\hat P$ is measurable with respect to the first phase, and mixing gives
$q_t(s)\ge\gamma\bar p(s)\ge\gamma/(\kappa|\Sset|)$ at every later round, so
$\rho_t\le\kappa|\Sset|\max_{s,a}|\hat P(s\mid a)-P(s\mid a)|/\gamma\le\bar\rho\le\tfrac12$
for every $t>n_0$ and every realization of the second phase. Proposition~\ref{prop:plugin}
therefore applies at every such round, and conditionally on the first phase the second is exactly
the algorithm of Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3} driven by a nonnegative estimate with weighted second
moment at most $2\hat v_t\le2|\Sset|$. Its proof gives
$\Ex[\sum_{t>n_0}(\langle p_t,\bar c_t\rangle-\bar c_t(a^\star))]\le(d+1)\log K/\eta+2\eta|\Sset|T$,
the extra factor of two coming from $p_t\le x_t/(1-\gamma)\le2x_t$. Playing $x_t$ rather than $p_t$ costs at most $\gamma$ per round since $c_t\in[0,1]^K$, so it
remains to pass from $\bar c_t$ to $c_t$ under $p_t$. Proposition~\ref{prop:plugin} gives
$\langle x_t,b_t\rangle=0$ for the per-action bias $b_t$, and $x_t=(1-\gamma)p_t+\gamma u$ for
the uniform $u$, so $\langle p_t,b_t\rangle=-\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\langle u,b_t\rangle$, which
for $\gamma\le\tfrac12$ is at most $\max_a|b_t(a)|$ in absolute value. Each round therefore adds
$|\langle p_t,b_t\rangle|+|b_t(a^\star)|\le2\max_a|b_t(a)|\le4\bar\varepsilon+4\bar\rho$. Collecting the four contributions gives
the display. For the rate, balancing $\gamma T$ against $2T\bar\rho$ gives the stated $\gamma$ and
a combined $\Ot(T(\kappa|\Sset|)^{1/2}(K/n_0)^{1/4})$, and balancing that against $n_0$ gives the
stated $n_0$, under which $T\bar\varepsilon$ and the constraint on $n_0$ are of lower order.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Safe blending.}
Blending the plug-in with the action-level estimate makes the guarantee safe. Let
$\lambda_t\in[0,1]^K$ be $\mathcal F_{t-1}$-measurable and put
$\check g_t(a)=(1-\lambda_t(a))\tilde c_t(a)+\lambda_t(a)\hat c_t(a)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:blend}
$\check g_t\ge0$, its conditional mean satisfies
$\Ex[\check g_t(a)\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]-c_t(a)=\lambda_t(a)\,(\bar c_t(a)-c_t(a))$
for the plug-in's conditional mean $\bar c_t$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:plugin}, and
\[
\sum_ax_t(a)\Ex[\check g_t(a)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]\ \le\ (K-\Pi_t)+\hat V_t ,
\]
for $\Pi_t=\sum_a\lambda_t(a)$ and
$\hat V_t=\sum_a\lambda_t(a)x_t(a)\Ex[\hat c_t(a)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]$, which satisfies
$\hat V_t\le2\hat v_t$ on $\{\rho_t\le\tfrac12\}$ and not in general, since the step
$q_t/\hat q_t\le2$ behind it is exactly that event. We write $V^{\mathrm{ub}}_t(\tau)$ below for a
computable upper bound on $\hat V_t$, reserving $\hat V_t$ for the conditional expectation
itself.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Both parts are nonnegative and $\lambda_t(a)\in[0,1]$. The mean identity is linearity together
with $\Ex[\tilde c_t(a)\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=c_t(a)$. For the second moment, $u\mapsto u^2$ is
convex, so $\check g_t(a)^2\le(1-\lambda_t(a))\tilde c_t(a)^2+\lambda_t(a)\hat c_t(a)^2$, and
$x_t(a)\Ex[\tilde c_t(a)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]\le1$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:pool}.
\end{proof}

So a weight of $\lambda_t(a)$ on the plug-in buys a variance saving of $\lambda_t(a)$ and pays a
bias of $\lambda_t(a)$ times the plug-in's, and both endpoints are exact. Let $r_t(a)$ and $\hat\rho_t$ be any sequences computable from the immediate pairs that are
\emph{simultaneously valid}, meaning $\varepsilon_t(a)\le r_t(a)$ for every $a$ and
$\rho_t\le\hat\rho_t$ at every $t\le T$, on an event of probability at least $1-1/T$. Write
$V^{\mathrm{ub}}_t(\tau)=2\sum_{a:r_t(a)\le\tau}x_t(a)\sum_s\hat P_t(s\mid a)^2/\hat q_t(s)$,
which the learner can evaluate and which dominates $\hat V_t$ on $\{\rho_t\le\tfrac12\}$. \textsc{Safe-Pool State-EXP3} sets $\lambda_t\equiv0$ when
$\hat\rho_t>\tfrac12$, and otherwise picks the best member of the candidate set
$\{\bot\}\cup\{r_t(a):a\le K\}$, where $\bot$ stands for $\lambda_t\equiv0$ and a numerical
candidate $\tau$ stands for $\lambda_t(a)=\mathbf 1\{r_t(a)\le\tau\}$, best meaning smallest
\[
\Psi_t(\tau)=\tfrac\eta2\bigl[(K-\Pi_t(\tau))+V^{\mathrm{ub}}_t(\tau)\bigr]
+\bigl(4\tau+4\hat\rho_t\bigr)\mathbf 1\{\Pi_t(\tau)>0\},
\qquad\Psi_t(\bot)=\tfrac\eta2K.
\]
Write $\Psi^\star_t$ for that smallest value. The candidate $\bot$ is listed apart from the
numerical thresholds because a valid radius may equal zero, in which case $\tau=0$ pools the
actions attaining it rather than abstaining, so $\bot$ rather than $\tau=0$ is what keeps
$\tfrac\eta2K$ available on every round.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:safe}
For every $\eta>0$, \textup{\textsc{Safe-Pool State-EXP3}} with $m=d+1$ satisfies
\[
\Ex[R_T]\ \le\ 1+\frac{(d+1)\log K}{\eta}
+\Ex\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^T\Psi^\star_t\Bigr],
\]
so $\eta=\sqrt{2(d+1)\log K/(KT)}$ gives $\Ex[R_T]\le1+\sqrt{2(d+1)KT\log K}$ on every instance,
which is the guarantee of action-level exponential weights, with an improvement on each round
whose $\Psi^\star_t$ is strictly below $\tfrac\eta2K$. The certificate is computed from the
learner's own observations and so is random, which is why the display carries an expectation.
The tuned bound does not, because $\Psi^\star_t\le\tfrac\eta2K$ holds on every path.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On the validity event, of probability at least $1-1/T$ and contributing at most $1$ off it,
pooling happens only when $\hat\rho_t\le\tfrac12$ and hence only when $\rho_t\le\tfrac12$, which
is what makes $V^{\mathrm{ub}}_t$ dominate the conditional second moment. Proposition~\ref{prop:blend}
then supplies a nonnegative estimate whose weighted second moment is at most
$(K-\Pi_t)+\hat V_t$, so the argument of Appendix~\ref{app:etc} applies to the surrogate
losses. Passing to $c_t$ adds both the played bias
$|\sum_ax_t(a)\lambda_t(a)b_t(a)|$ and the comparator bias $\lambda_t(a^\star)|b_t(a^\star)|$,
for $b_t=\bar c_t-c_t$.
The cancellation of Proposition~\ref{prop:plugin} is lost under per-action weights, as the
counterexample there shows, so each is bounded separately. On a pooled action
$\varepsilon_t(a)\le r_t(a)\le\tau$ gives $|b_t(a)|\le2\tau+2\hat\rho_t$, and since
$\sum_ax_t(a)\lambda_t(a)\le1$ and $\lambda_t(a^\star)\le1$ the two together are at most $4\tau+4\hat\rho_t$, charged only when something is pooled. The
second-moment bound and the two bias terms are all functions of the observations, so the
per-round costs they assemble into are the random $\Psi^\star_t$ and the sum is taken in
expectation. For the tuned bound, $\bot$ belongs to the candidate set and
$\Psi_t(\bot)=\tfrac\eta2K$, so $\Psi^\star_t\le\tfrac\eta2K$ on every path whatever the radii
are.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why certification stays conservative.}
\textbf{The guarantee is safe and the algorithm is inert.} Pooling is certified when
$4(\tau+\hat\rho_t)\le\tfrac\eta2(\Pi_t-V^{\mathrm{ub}}_t)$. At the tuned $\eta$ both sides
fall as $T^{-1/2}$, the left through $\tau\asymp\sqrt{K\log(K|\Sset|T)/T}$ under uniform play and
the right through $\eta\asymp\sqrt{\log K/(KT)}$. Their ratio is
$\Theta(\sqrt{\log(K|\Sset|T)/\log K})$, which exceeds one and does not vanish with the horizon,
growing slowly in $T$ through the logarithmic factors, so no horizon makes the certificate
clear. Appendix~\ref{app:num} reports behavior consistent with
this explanation, the algorithm reproducing action-level regret to the digit with a pooled
fraction of zero.

The obstruction is not the shape of the bias bound. Regret charges
$\langle x_t,b_t\rangle-b_t(a^\star)$ for the per-action bias $b_t$, while
Proposition~\ref{prop:plugin} charges $\max_a|b_t(a)|$. Measured against the budget itself at
$K=40$, $|\Sset|=8$, $T=8000$ and $d=50$, the first is $0.42$ times it and the second $1.67$ times
it, so on that trajectory the realized bias lies below the best-case variance budget and a tighter
bias bound would close the analytical gap. Two exact refinements fail to deliver one. Both
$\sum_s\Delta_t(s\mid a)$ and $\sum_s\delta_t(s)$ vanish, so a constant may be subtracted and a
maximum replaced by an oscillation, and Cauchy--Schwarz against $q_t$ replaces the comparator term
by $\sqrt{\nu_t(a^\star)}\,\chi_t$ for its own effective dimension $\nu_t$ and a chi-square
$\chi_t$ between $\hat q_t$ and $q_t$. Measured, these are $6.1$ and $63$ times the truth against
$3.9$ for the maximum they replace.

What the algorithm can verify from its own counts is $104$ times the budget, and Bernstein radii do
not improve on Hoeffding ones (Appendix~\ref{app:num}), so the barrier is certifying $\rho_t$, the
relative error the estimate induces on the state distribution. The play-weighted identity of
Proposition~\ref{prop:plugin} says the whole charge is the comparator's, and it routes through
$\rho_t$ only because $\hat q_t=x_t^\top\hat P_t$ predicts the state distribution out of every row
of $P$. Predicting is expensive for that reason, needing
$t=\Omega(K|\Sset|\log(K|\Sset|T)/\rho^2)$ rounds, and the resulting certificate clears for no
$K\le T$, since it would need $\log K\ge16c|\Sset|$ for $c=\log(2|\Sset|T)$, which $\log K\le\log
T$ cannot meet.

Counting the states costs less and still buys nothing. With $x_t$ frozen over a window the
empirical state frequency is a plain multinomial estimate of $q_t$, needing
$n=\Omega(|\Sset|\log(|\Sset|T)/\rho^2)$ samples, a factor $K$ fewer than predicting and free of
$P$, and the certificate does clear once $K\log K\ge64c|\Sset|$, about $K\ge10^3$ at $|\Sset|=8$.
The window that buys it must satisfy $n\ge32c|\Sset|T/(K\log K)$, a constant fraction of the
horizon, and freezing the play over blocks of length $B$ costs $\sqrt{2TBK\log K}$, which at that
$B$ is $T\sqrt{128c|\Sset|}$ and so linear in $T$. Both routes fail for one reason: the certificate
must resolve $q_t$ to relative accuracy $\Theta(\sqrt{K\log K/T})$, finer than either estimate
delivers from the data the learner has.

In words, an unknown action-to-state matrix costs a bias in two accuracies, the $\ell_1$ error of $\hat P$ and the
relative error it induces on the state distribution, and only the second needs forced
exploration, which is what sets the rate. \textbf{The bound does not certify an improvement from the channel it analyzes.} Its second term exceeds $\sqrt{KT}$ at every horizon with $T>K$, by a factor
$(T/K)^{3/10}$, so a learner holding this guarantee alone should run action-level exponential
weights instead. The experiments suggest the analysis is conservative rather than the
algorithm weak, since Appendix~\ref{app:num} shows it tracking known $P$ closely, and sharpening
it is the natural next result.

\section{Feedback models, and what transfers}\label{app:transfer}

Write $\lamtwo=\sum_t\min\{1,\|c_t-m_t\|_2^2\}$ for the inaccuracy measure of
\citet{baron2025}. Unlike $\etwo$, which charges only the worst action in a round, $\lamtwo$
grows with how many actions are predicted wrongly, and since
$\|v\|_\infty\le\|v\|_2\le\sqrt q\|v\|_\infty$ for a vector $v$ with $q$ nonzero
coordinates, $\etwo\le\lamtwo\le q\,\etwo$ with both ends attained.

\citet{baron2025} analyze a model in which the played coordinate of the loss vector is observed
exactly. Section~\ref{sec:setting} instead observes a random outcome whose conditional mean is
the loss. The following records precisely what survives the substitution.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:moments}
Take the action-level $\tilde c_t(a)=\mathbf 1\{A_t=a\}X_t/x_t(a)$ of
Proposition~\ref{prop:pool} under the model of Section~\ref{sec:setting}. Then for every action
$a$, (i) $\Ex[\tilde c_t(a)\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=c_t(a)$, (ii) $0\le\tilde c_t(a)\le 1/x_t(a)$,
and (iii) $\Ex[\tilde c_t(a)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]\le 1/x_t(a)$. The centered estimator
$\check c_t(a)=m_t(a)+\mathbf 1\{A_t=a\}(X_t-m_t(a))/x_t(a)$ also satisfies (i), and its residual
obeys $\Ex[(\check c_t(a)-m_t(a))^2\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]\le 1/x_t(a)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For (i), by the tower rule $\Ex[\mathbf 1\{A_t=a\}X_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]
=x_t(a)\,\Ex[X_t\mid A_t=a]=x_t(a)c_t(a)$, and dividing by $x_t(a)$ gives the claim.
Item (ii) holds because $X_t\in[0,1]$. For (iii),
$\Ex[\tilde c_t(a)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=\Ex[X_t^2\mathbf 1\{A_t=a\}]/x_t(a)^2
=\Ex[X_t^2\mid A_t=a]/x_t(a)\le 1/x_t(a)$ because $X_t\le1$. The statements for $\check c_t$ follow the same computation with $X_t$ replaced by $X_t-m_t(a)$,
using $|X_t-m_t(a)|\le1$.
\end{proof}

In words, replacing an exactly observed loss by a bounded unbiased draw of it changes neither the
mean of the estimator nor the moment bounds an analysis consumes.

Under exact feedback the corresponding estimator satisfies (i) to (iii) with identical
bounds, since $c_t(a)\le1$. Consequently any step of an exact-feedback analysis that uses the
observation only through unbiasedness and these two moment bounds is unchanged under noisy
outcomes. What this does not cover is the comparison between the estimate built from the stale
action-loss vector and the estimate built from the true loss. Under exact feedback that difference is
determined by the played action. Under noisy outcomes it carries an additional mean-zero term of
magnitude up to $1/x_t(A_t)$, and bounding its cumulative effect over a window of $d$ rounds is
what requires the stability property falsified in Appendix~\ref{app:counter}. An upper bound for
the noisy model is Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3}. What remains open is one adaptive to $\etwo$.

\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}

The measure $\etwo$ is controlled by the state-loss variation $W$, and no converse bound
holds in general.

\begin{lemma}[Delay window]\label{lem:window}
$\etwo\le d^2W$ for $W=\sum_t\|\theta_t-\theta_{t-1}\|_\infty^2$. The ratio $\etwo/(d^2W)$ tends to one when $\theta$ drifts monotonically in one coordinate at a
constant rate and $T/d\to\infty$, the first
$d$ rounds contributing $\sum_{j<d}j^2$ rather than $d^2$ each under the convention $m_t=c_1$.
\end{lemma}

In words, accumulated squared prediction error is at most the state-loss variation inflated by
the square of the delay, and no better bound in $W$ alone is available.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:window}]
Throughout write $\Delta_j=\theta_j-\theta_{j-1}$, set $\theta_0=\theta_1$, so $\Delta_1=0$, and read
$c_r$ as $c_1$ for $r\le0$, matching the convention $m_t=c_1$ for $t\le d$ of
Section~\ref{sec:setting}. Then $c_t-c_{t-d}=P\sum_{j=t-d+1}^t\Delta_j$. Because the
rows of $P$ are probability vectors, averaging cannot increase the supremum norm, so
$\|c_t-c_{t-d}\|_\infty\le\sum_j\|\Delta_j\|_\infty$. By Cauchy--Schwarz applied to the $d$
summands, $\|c_t-c_{t-d}\|_\infty^2\le d\sum_{j=t-d+1}^t\|\Delta_j\|_\infty^2$. Summing over $t$
and using that each increment belongs to at most $d$ windows gives $\etwo\le d^2W$. The ratio $\etwo/(d^2W)$ approaches one when every increment has the same magnitude, sign and
active coordinate, and some action reaches that coordinate deterministically, since then no
cancellation occurs within a full window and $P$ passes the increment through undiminished.
Without the second condition the drift can lie in the kernel of $P$ and the ratio can be zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Information isolation]\label{lem:iso}
If $h\le d$ then $A_t$ is independent of the signs governing its own block, and this remains true
when the learner is provided with the exact stale action-loss vector $m_t$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:iso}]
For $t$ in block $b$, every realized outcome usable when choosing $A_t$ comes from a round
$r\le t-d-1$. Since $t\le bh$ and $h\le d$, we have $r\le bh-d-1<(b-1)h$, so every usable realized
outcome depends only on signs from blocks strictly before $b$, whose joint law depends only on
$\sigma_2,\dots,\sigma_{b-1}$. Hence by conditional
independence $A_t$ is independent of block $b$'s signs. For the oracle, take any $h\le d$. If
$t>d$ then $t-d\le bh-d\le(b-1)h$ by the same computation, so $m_t=c_{t-d}$ is measurable with
respect to the signs of blocks strictly before $b$. If $t\le d$ then $m_t=c_1=\tfrac12\mathbf 1$,
which is deterministic because block~$1$ is neutral. In both cases $m_t$ is independent of block
$b$'s signs, so adjoining it to the learner's information leaves the conclusion intact.
\end{proof}

The construction in full. Take states $s_0,\dots,s_q$, a stationary deterministic map sending
$a_j$ to $s_j$ for $j\le q$ and every other action to $s_0$, and independent Rademacher signs
$\sigma_b^{(j)}$ for $2\le b\le B=\lfloor T/h\rfloor$. Set $\theta_t(s_0)=\tfrac12$ throughout,
$\theta_t\equiv\tfrac12$ on block~$1$, and $\theta_t(s_j)=\tfrac12-\varepsilon\sigma_b^{(j)}$ on
each later block $b$. The neutral first block makes $c_1=\tfrac12\mathbf 1$, so the
convention $m_t=c_1$ for $t\le d$ of Section~\ref{sec:setting} supplies a constant over exactly
the rounds that precede any usable feedback, which is what Lemma~\ref{lem:iso} needs at $b=1$. Rounds after the last complete block carry no drift, costing a constant factor.

\begin{lemma}[Rademacher lower tail]\label{lem:rade}
Let $S_B$ be a sum of $B\ge32$ independent Rademacher signs. For every $1\le u\le\sqrt B/32$,
$\Pr(S_B\ge u\sqrt B)\ge\tfrac u4e^{-64u^2}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $S_B=2X-B$ with $X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(B,\tfrac12)$, so that
$\{S_B\ge u\sqrt B\}=\{X\ge B/2+u\sqrt B/2\}$. Passing to $X$ removes the lattice gap, since $X$
takes every integer value in $[0,B]$ whereas $S_B$ takes only those of the parity of $B$. Put
$n=\lfloor B/2\rfloor$ and $t=\lceil u\sqrt B/2\rceil+1$, so $X\ge n+t$ implies the event, and
$t\le u\sqrt B$ because $u\sqrt B\ge\sqrt{32}>4$.

For $j\ge1$ the ratio of consecutive binomial probabilities is
$\Pr(X=n+j)/\Pr(X=n+j-1)=(B-n-j+1)/(n+j)$. Using $(B-1)/2\le n\le B/2$, its distance from one is
at most $(2j-1)/(n+j)\le(4j-2)/(B-1)\le8j/B$ for $B\ge2$. Restricting to $j\le B/16$ keeps
$8j/B\le\tfrac12$, where $\log(1-x)\ge-2x$ holds, so summing over $i\le j$ gives
$\Pr(X=n+j)\ge\Pr(X=n)e^{-16j(j+1)/(2B)}\ge\Pr(X=n)e^{-16j^2/B}$. The central term satisfies
$\Pr(X=n)\ge1/(2\sqrt B)$ for every $B\ge2$.

Sum over the $t$ integers $j\in\{t,\dots,2t-1\}$, all of which are at most $2t$ and hence at most
$B/16$ because $u\le\sqrt B/32$ forces $2t\le2u\sqrt B\le B/16$. Each term is at least
$e^{-64t^2/B}/(2\sqrt B)$, and $t\le u\sqrt B$ makes the exponent at most $64u^2$, so
$\Pr(X\ge n+t)\ge t\,e^{-64u^2}/(2\sqrt B)\ge\tfrac u4e^{-64u^2}$ using $t\ge u\sqrt B/2$.
\end{proof}

Three regimes give the maximal inequality the lower bound needs. Each $S_B^{(j)}$ is sub-Gaussian
with variance proxy $B$, so $\Ex\max_j(S_B^{(j)})^{+}\le\sqrt{2B\log q}+\sqrt B$ throughout. For
the matching lower bound, first suppose $\log q\le B/8$ and $\log q\ge128$. Taking
$u=\sqrt{\log q/128}$, which then lies in $[1,\sqrt B/32]$, makes $e^{-64u^2}=q^{-1/2}$, so
$\Pr(S_B^{(j)}\ge u\sqrt B)\ge\tfrac u4q^{-1/2}$ and independence across $j$ gives
$\Pr(\max_jS_B^{(j)}<u\sqrt B)\le e^{-u\sqrt q/4}$, at most $\tfrac12$ beyond an absolute $q$.
Hence $\Ex\max_j(S_B^{(j)})^{+}\ge\tfrac12u\sqrt B=\Omega(\sqrt{B\log q})$. Second, if
$\log q>B/8$ and $B\ge1024$, take $u=\sqrt B/32$, so $u\sqrt B=B/32$ and $e^{-64u^2}=e^{-B/16}$,
and $q\ge e^{B/8}$ makes $q\,e^{-B/16}$ diverge, giving
$\Ex\max_j(S_B^{(j)})^{+}=\Omega(B)$, which is $\Omega(\sqrt{B\min\{1+\log q,B\}})$ in that
regime. Third, when $\log q<128$ or $B<1024$ the quantity $\sqrt{B\min\{1+\log q,B\}}$ is
$O(\sqrt B)$, and a single walk already gives
$\Ex(S_B^{(1)})^{+}=\tfrac12\Ex|S_B|\ge\tfrac12(\Ex S_B^2)^{3/2}(\Ex S_B^4)^{-1/2}\ge\tfrac12\sqrt{B/3}$
by H\"older, since $\Ex S_B^2=B$ and $\Ex S_B^4\le3B^2$. Combining the three regimes,
$\Ex\max_j(S_B^{(j)})^{+}=\Theta(\sqrt{B\min\{1+\log q,B\}})$. This is the route by which maxima
of independent random walks fix the minimax rate for prediction with expert advice
\citep{cesabianchilugosi2006}.

\textbf{Proof idea.} Divide time into blocks of length $d$ and give $q$ actions private states
whose losses are perturbed by independent Rademacher signs within each block. Every observation
available when an action is chosen comes from an earlier block, and the supplied $m_t=c_{t-d}$
depends only on earlier signs, so the learner cannot predict the signs governing its current
block. Its expected loss is therefore $T/2$, while the best fixed action in hindsight benefits
from the maximum of $q$ independent random walks. Choosing the amplitude to make $\etwo\le\mathcal E$
gives the stated scale.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}]
Take $h=d$ and $\varepsilon=\tfrac12\sqrt{\mathcal E/T}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:iso} every algorithm's
expected loss equals $T/2$, since its action is independent of the current block's signs and all states have mean
$\tfrac12$ marginally. Its regret is therefore exactly the comparator's
fluctuation advantage,
$\varepsilon d\,\Ex\bigl[\max_{j\le q}\bigl(\sum_{2\le b\le B}\sigma_b^{(j)}\bigr)^{+}\bigr]$ with
$B=\lfloor T/d\rfloor$, over the $B-1$ randomized blocks. Per round $\|c_t-m_t\|_\infty\le2\varepsilon$, because the supremum norm charges only the largest
coordinate and each coordinate moves by at most $2\varepsilon$ at a block boundary; the gap is
$\varepsilon$ across the first randomized block, where $m_t$ is still the neutral $c_1$, and lies
in $\{0,2\varepsilon\}$ thereafter. Hence $\etwo\le4\varepsilon^2T=\mathcal E$ holds
deterministically. The
budget must bind on the realized sequence, since a budget holding only in expectation would not
constrain the instance selected by Yao's principle. By Lemma~\ref{lem:rade} and the discussion following it, the expected maximum of the positive
parts of $q$ independent Rademacher sums of length $B-1$ is
$\Theta(\sqrt{(B-1)\min\{1+\log q,B-1\}})$, which covers both regimes and the case $q=1$. The
hypothesis $d\le T/2$ gives $B\ge2$ and hence $B-1\ge B/2$, so replacing $B-1$ by $B$ costs a
factor at most $\sqrt2$ in each branch of the minimum, including the saturated branch where the
minimum is $B-1$ rather than $1+\log q$. Substituting $B=\lfloor T/d\rfloor$ and $\varepsilon$
therefore gives $\Theta(\sqrt{d\mathcal E\min\{1+\log q,T/d\}})$. Rounds after the last complete block
carry no drift and change the constant only. When the oracle is withheld, the $\sqrt{KT}$ term is \citet[Thm.~5.1]{esposito2023}, whose
construction is stationary and therefore lies in the present model class.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tight}
Suppose $X_t=c_t(A_t)$. On instances whose drift is carried by a single action, $\lamtwo=\etwo$,
so the bound of \citet{baron2025} reads $\Ot(\sqrt{KT+d\,\etwo})$ and its drift contribution
$\sqrt{d\,\etwo}$ matches Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} up to logarithmic factors.
\end{proposition}

In words, single-action drift makes the Euclidean and supremum measures agree, so on that family
the drift term of the published upper bound is unimprovable in order. Its $\sqrt{KT}$ term is not
matched because the oracle supplies an entire stale loss vector, so the oracle-assisted problem
need not retain bandit exploration complexity. Neither result provides an $\etwo$-adaptive upper
bound under noisy outcomes.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:tight}]
If only one coordinate of $c_t-m_t$ is nonzero then $\|c_t-m_t\|_2=\|c_t-m_t\|_\infty$. On the
family of Section~\ref{sec:lower} that coordinate has magnitude at most $2\varepsilon\le1$, so
the truncation at one in $\lamtwo$ is inactive and $\lamtwo=\etwo$. The bound of \citet{baron2025} then reads $\Ot(\sqrt{KT+d\etwo})$, whose $\sqrt{d\etwo}$
contribution Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} matches up to the logarithmic factor at $q=1$. Its
$\sqrt{KT}$ contribution is not matched.
\end{proof}

\section{The two-action counterexample}\label{app:counter}

Take $K=2$ with the hybrid regularizer of \citet{zimmert2020}, Tsallis parameter $\beta=0.01$,
learning rate $\eta=0.5$ and residual scale $C=1$. The iterate solves the stationarity condition
$g(x_1)+L_1=g(x_2)+L_2$ with $x_1+x_2=1$, where
\[
g(x)=-\tfrac{1}{2\beta}x^{-1/2}+\tfrac{1}{\eta}\log x
\]
and $L_a$ is the cumulative estimate for action $a$. Suppose the cumulative estimates place
action~1 at probability $x_1$. A single maximally negative centered residual $-C/x_1$ is then
delivered to action~1. This is realizable in the model of Section~\ref{sec:setting}, since
$m_t(1)=1$ and $X_t=0$ give exactly $\check c_t(1)-m_t(1)=-C/x_1$ with $C=1$. Solving the
stationarity condition again gives the following.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
$x_1$ before & $x_1$ after & ratio \\
\midrule
$9.15\times10^{-3}$ & $1.46\times10^{-2}$ & $1.6$ \\
$9.83\times10^{-4}$ & $7.39\times10^{-3}$ & $7.5$ \\
$2.19\times10^{-4}$ & $9.98\times10^{-1}$ & $4561$ \\
$5.80\times10^{-5}$ & $1.00$ & $17226$ \\
$4.86\times10^{-6}$ & $1.00$ & $205641$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The transition sits at $4\beta^2C^2=4\times10^{-4}$, which is where the Tsallis term
$x^{-1/2}$ ceases to dominate the entropy term $\log x$ in $g$, so the one-step linearization
underlying a constant-factor bound fails. The violated statement is the local-stability
inequality asserting that $x_{t'}(a)/x_t(a)$ stays bounded by a constant for all $t'$ within $d$
rounds of $t$. A single round refutes it, so no accumulation argument is needed. This invalidates that lemma
and the proof route resting on it, and not every argument that might use the same regularizer. It does not refute
Conjecture~\ref{conj:add}, which concerns the regret rate and may be provable by other means.
Devices that bound the residual magnitude introduce either a first-order bias dominating the
target term or a probability floor narrowing the delay regime.

Section~\ref{sec:setting}. Reported $\pm$ is one standard error of the paired difference. Throughout this appendix,
\emph{sample-path pseudo-regret} means $\sum_tc_t(A_t)-\min_a\sum_tc_t(a)$, evaluated on one
realization of the learner and environment randomness. Averaging it over seeds estimates the
expected pseudo-regret that the theorems bound. We use that name rather than ``realized regret'' to
distinguish it from regret computed from the sampled outcomes $X_t$, which is not what these
studies report.

\textbf{Does pooling help because the rows overlap, or because overlap made the task easy?}
Study~6 sweeps the overlap and finds the advantage falling as $\vhat$ falls, which read at face
value contradicts Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3}, whose bound improves as $\bar v$ falls. That sweep is
confounded, since raising the overlap also flattens $c_t=P\theta_t$ across actions, collapsing
the comparator gap so that both regrets vanish together. This study holds $K=40$, $|\Sset|=8$,
$T=10000$, $d=50$ and the difficulty fixed, and varies only $\vhat$. Difficulty is the
uniform-play regret $\sum_t\mathrm{mean}_ac_t(a)-\min_a\sum_tc_t(a)$, a property of the instance
with no algorithm in it, and the amplitude of $\theta$ is bisected in every cell to hit a common
target. The map keeps one contrast direction alive at every overlap and $\theta$ is aligned with
it, which is what lets the gap survive as the rows agree.

Observed. Across $20$ paired seeds the relative gain rises from $11.7$ per cent at
$\vhat=3.380$ to $19.3$ per cent at $\vhat=1.120$, with absolute gains $85.5\pm8.9$ to

sweep falls from $34.5$ to $3.1$ per cent over a comparable range (Figure~\ref{fig:matched}).

Interpretation. The two sweeps disagree in sign, and the matched-difficulty sweep agrees with the overlap
dependence suggested by Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3}, although this experiment uses the practical
$m=1$ variant rather than the theorem's $m=d+1$ algorithm. Overlap is what pooling exploits, and study~6
measures overlap confounded with an instance becoming trivial. This also explains study~9's
degenerate column without appealing to it.

Limitation. At the two highest overlaps some seeds cannot reach the common target and the
realized difficulty is $5$ to $13$ per cent below it, so the matching is close rather than exact.
Since a smaller gap should if anything shrink the advantage, the measured rise is conservative in
that direction. The matched gains are also smaller in magnitude than the unmatched ones, which is
what fixing the difficulty costs.

every round are measured on the same paired seeds across $K$ from $8$ to $160$, and drawn beside
the two certificates. Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3} is the certificate for known $P$ and analyzes
$m=d+1$, while Theorem~\ref{thm:unknown} is the warm-start rate and analyzes an algorithm that
freezes $\hat P$ and restarts, so both are plotted to show the size of a gap and not as like for
like.

Observed. The plug-in stays within $1.6$ per cent of known $P$ at every $K$, the two curves being
visually indistinguishable, while action-level weighting costs $1.4$ to $17.4$ per cent more
than the plug-in as $K$ rises twentyfold. Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3} sits $10.7$ to $14.8$ times
above the measured plug-in and Theorem~\ref{thm:unknown} sits $14.0$ to $18.7$ times above it.
The warm-start certificate also stays above $\sqrt{KT}$ at every $K$ tried, by factors of $19.2$
down to $6.6$.

Interpretation. The vertical gap reflects analytical conservatism rather than observed algorithmic performance. Estimating $P$ online is close
to free on this family, so the order of magnitude between the measured arms and either
certificate is conservatism in the proof. That the warm-start rate never drops below $\sqrt{KT}$
here is the concrete form of the statement in Section~\ref{sec:alg} that it does not certify the
channel it analyzes.

Limitation. The measured arms run $m=1$ and Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3} analyzes $m=d+1$, and the
plug-in is not the warm-start algorithm Theorem~\ref{thm:unknown} analyzes, so neither vertical
gap is a statement about a bound being loose for the algorithm it covers. A conservative bound and
a well-behaved algorithm are consistent with a third possibility, that the family is easy.

\textbf{Can the coarsening be chosen from data?} Theorem~\ref{thm:rep} takes the representation
as given and study~7 uses the latent cluster count, which no algorithm knows. This study supplies
a rule and measures what it costs, with no theorem behind it. A prefix of $1200$ rounds runs the
identity representation and estimates $\hat\theta(s)$ by averaging the outcomes seen at each
state. Sorting states by $\hat\theta$ and merging adjacent pairs greedily gives one candidate at
every granularity from $16$ down to $1$, each scored by the quantity Theorem~\ref{thm:rep}
bounds, $\sqrt{2(d+1)V(g)\log K}+2\sum_t\hat\delta_t(g)$, and the argmin runs the remaining
rounds. Against it are the identity, the true clusters, and the best candidate on the ladder
scored in hindsight, over $24$ paired seeds.

Observed. The rule fails. It selects $15$ groups at heterogeneity $0$ and $16$ at $0.12$, against
a true cluster count of four, and lands at $912.9\pm30.1$ and $944.7\pm31.1$ where the identity
gives $921.0\pm30.5$ and $952.7\pm32.4$, so it captures under one per cent of the available gain.
The true clusters give $843.7\pm27.9$ and $883.0\pm31.1$, and hindsight over the same ladder gives
$833.5\pm27.8$ and $841.5\pm23.6$.

Interpretation. Hindsight nearly matches the true clusters, so the ladder does contain a good
representation and the failure is the criterion rather than the candidate set. Measuring
$\hat\delta$ at the four-group representation locates it. At heterogeneity $0$ the true
within-group spread is exactly zero while the prefix estimates it at $0.154$, which is sampling
noise, and the bound multiplies that by $2(T-T_0)$ to give a phantom bias of $1483$ against a real
variance saving of $1261$. The bias term is the conservative half of Theorem~\ref{thm:rep}, and a
noisy plug-in estimate of it is enough to forbid every merge.

Limitation. One selector on one family, and a negative result about this rule is not a negative
result about data-driven selection. The obvious repairs, debiasing $\hat\delta$ for its sampling
noise or scoring candidates on held-out loss instead of on the bound, are not evaluated here. The
prefix is also charged to every arm equally, so the comparison understates what a selector that
spent less on estimation might do.

\textbf{Is ``many actions resolved by few states'' a natural regime?} Every study so far draws
$P$ from a Dirichlet, which is the assumption under test rather than evidence for it. This one
builds an environment to the shape of a recommendation funnel instead. Actions are $K=200$
catalogue items, states are $|\Sset|=6$ ordered engagement depths, and the outcome is a delayed
conversion. Three structural facts are imposed because funnels have them, namely that items fall
into a modest number of categories sharing an engagement profile, that item quality is
heavy-tailed, and that the loss falls with engagement depth and drifts seasonally rather than
adversarially. The generated catalogue puts $74$ per cent of its mass on no engagement and $1.4$
per cent on the deepest.

Observed. The effective dimension is $\vhat=1.97$ against $K=200$, a hundredfold gap, and it
stays between $1.41$ and $1.81$ as the catalogue grows from $25$ to $800$ items
(Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}). Against action-level weighting the state-pooled variant saves $79.3$,
$63.6$ and $38.9$ per cent at $d=10$, $50$ and $200$, the largest margins anywhere in this
appendix. The plug-in matches known $P$ at every delay, the paired difference never reaching two
standard errors. The regime condition $\bar v(d+1)\log K\le K+d$ holds at $d=10$ and fails at
$d=50$ and $d=200$.

Interpretation. The separation between $K$ and $\vhat$ arrives from catalogue structure rather
than from a tuned parameter, and it does not close as the catalogue grows, which is the claim the
Dirichlet families cannot make. That the condition Theorem~\ref{thm:sexp3} needs fails at the
delays a funnel has, while the practical variant still saves most of the regret, is the same
message as study~1 in the setting that motivates the paper. It is evidence for
Conjecture~\ref{conj:add} rather than for the theorem.

Limitation. \emph{No real data is used.} There is no interaction log in this environment, nothing
here is fitted to one, and this is not a semi-synthetic benchmark in the usual sense of that
phrase. The structure is imposed by hand from qualitative properties, so it shows that the regime
is describable and self-consistent, not that any deployed system occupies it. The arms run $m=1$,
which carries no guarantee, and the environment has no adversarial component, so it exercises the
pooling channel and not the drift channel.

\textbf{Which assumption produces which benefit?} Comparing against action-level weighting alone
leaves open whether the gain comes from the state structure or from an implementation detail.
We run five policies on the same paired seeds over nine settings. Besides action-level weighting
and \textsc{State-EXP3} they are \textsc{EXP3-IX} \citep{neu2015}, the implicit-exploration variant of \textsc{EXP3}, an importance
weight; \emph{naive sharing}, which credits $P(S_t\mid a)X_t/\bar p(S_t)$ using the unconditional
state distribution and so isolates the value of the denominator $q_t=x_t^\top P$ from the value
of sharing at all; and a \emph{best-state rule}, which estimates $\hat\theta(s)$ online, takes the
best state, and plays the action most likely to reach it. Two settings are added to the earlier
families, one where the best action mixes over several good states rather than maximizing the
single best one, and one that is obliviously nonstationary. Running a bandit over states and
mapping the values back through $P$ is not a separate baseline, since
$\sum_sP(s\mid a)\mathbf 1\{S_t=s\}X_t/q_t(s)$ equals $P(S_t\mid a)X_t/q_t(S_t)$ identically, so
that method \emph{is} \textsc{State-EXP3}.

Observed. \textsc{State-EXP3} does not win most columns. The best-state rule leads seven of the
nine and \textsc{State-EXP3} leads two, and over these nine settings the rule is also better on
the median, $1.00$ against $1.47$ times the column winner, and on the worst case, $3.08$ against
$3.39$ (Figure~\ref{fig:base}). \textsc{EXP3-IX} is $1.5$ to $17.1$ per cent worse than plain
action-level weighting everywhere, and naive sharing ranges from $18.7$ per cent better to $22.7$
per cent worse than it. On the spread family the ordering inverts, \textsc{State-EXP3} leading by
$47.6$ and $22.2$ per cent while the best-state rule falls $61.5$ and $43.0$ per cent behind
action-level weighting. Sweeping the horizon there, the rule's regret grows with fitted exponent
$1.00$ and \textsc{State-EXP3}'s with $0.58$.

Interpretation. The best-state rule buys its lead with an assumption, that the best action is the
one most likely to reach the single best state. Where that holds it is very strong, and in the
funnel it holds in $10$ of $10$ seeds because engagement depth is ordered, which is why it leads
there by a factor of two. Where it fails the rule commits to a fixed action and pays regret linear
in $T$, which no exploration rate repairs, and the exponent of $1.00$ is that happening. The
comparison therefore separates the assumptions rather than ranking the methods.
\textsc{State-EXP3} assumes nothing about the shape of $\theta$. Across these nine selected
settings its largest regret ratio to the column winner is $3.39$, and the rule's suite maximum is
slightly smaller at $3.08$, but the rule's fitted growth on the spread family is linear.
Action-level weighting also carries a sublinear guarantee, so what separates the arms here is the
constant rather than the rate. \textsc{State-EXP3}'s paired standard errors are the smallest of
the five at $5.8$ per cent of the mean against $23.6$ for the rule. That naive sharing
can be worse than no sharing is the separate point that the denominator, not the sharing, is what
Proposition~\ref{prop:pool} buys.

Limitation. Nine settings chosen here are not a worst case over the model, and the median and
worst-case columns above should be read as describing this set only. A practitioner whose problem
has the ordered structure of the funnel should use the best-state rule, and this appendix does not
identify when that structure can be verified in advance. On the drift family no arm's paired difference from
action-level weighting reaches two standard errors, the largest being the best-state rule's
$+24.6\pm12.4$, so that column is reported as an absence of signal rather than a result.

Tsallis-plus-entropy \textsc{FTRL} of \citet{zimmert2020}, whose delay term is additive rather

\section{Why the delay does not become additive}\label{app:additive}

Run \textsc{State-EXP3} with $m=1$, so $x_t\propto\exp(-\eta L_t)$ with
$L_t=\sum_{r\le t-d-1}\hat c_r$. Let $\tilde x_r$ be the iterate that has also absorbed the
outstanding estimates $G_r=\sum_{s=r-d}^{r-1}\hat c_s\ge0$. Two terms appear.

The delay term is $\Ex[\langle x_r-\tilde x_r,\hat c_r\rangle]$. Since
$\tilde x_r(a)\ge x_r(a)e^{-\eta G_r(a)}$ and $1-e^{-z}\le z$, it is at most
$\eta\Ex[\sum_ax_r(a)G_r(a)\hat c_r(a)]=\eta\sum_{s=r-d}^{r-1}\Ex[\langle x_r,\hat c_s\rangle]$,
and $x_r$ depends only on rounds $r-d-1$ and earlier, hence on rounds before $s$, so
$\Ex[\langle x_r,\hat c_s\rangle]=\Ex[\langle x_r,c_s\rangle]\le1$. This term is $\eta dT$ for
any unbiased estimator, which is the additive form.

The quadratic term is where it stops. The telescoping scores
$\sum_ax_{r+d}(a)\hat c_r(a)^2$, with $x_{r+d}$ the iterate that has absorbed rounds up to $r-1$,
while Proposition~\ref{prop:pool} bounds the play-weighted sum, the one against $x_r$, by $v_r$.
Carrying out the substitution,
$\Ex[\sum_ax_{r+d}(a)\hat c_r(a)^2\mid\cdot]\le\sum_sq_{r+d}(s)/q_r(s)\le|\Sset|/Z_r$ for
$Z_r=\Ex_{a\sim x_r}[e^{-\eta G_r(a)}]$, and $Z_r\ge e^{-\eta\langle x_r,G_r\rangle}$, so the
swap costs an exponential moment of $\langle x_r,G_r\rangle$.

No bound on that moment holds uniformly over state distributions. For this estimator
$\langle x_r,\hat c_s\rangle=q_r(S_s)X_s/q_s(S_s)$, so the quantity is governed by
$1/q_s(S_s)$, the reciprocal probability of the state actually observed. Its mean is
$\sum_sq_s(s)/q_s(s)=|\Sset|$, which is why the delay term is fine. For a fixed $q$ of full
support the exponential moment is finite, $1/q(S)$ being bounded, and what fails is uniformity.
Already at $|\Sset|=2$ with $q=(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)$,
$\Ex[e^{\eta/q(S)}]\ge\varepsilon e^{\eta/\varepsilon}\to\infty$ as $\varepsilon\to0$ while the
mean stays at $2$, so no bound in $|\Sset|$ and $\eta$ alone is available, and $q_s$ is set by the
algorithm's own past play. Measured at $|\Sset|=4$, the mean stays at $4.0$ while
$\Ex[e^{0.05/q(S)}]$ runs $1.22$, $1.43$ and then $1.3\times10^{21}$ as the smallest state
probability falls through $0.25$, $0.019$ and $0.0009$.

Flooring $q$ restores the moment and costs the product back. Mixing with the uniform action at
rate $\gamma$ gives $q_t(s)\ge\gamma\bar p(s)$, so $\eta\langle x_r,G_r\rangle\le1$ needs
$\gamma\asymp\eta d\kappa|\Sset|$ under the balanced-sensor condition $\bar p(s)\ge1/(\kappa|\Sset|)$,
and the mixing cost $\gamma T$ then reproduces $\Ot(\sqrt{d\kappa|\Sset|T\log K})$. So the
obstruction is not an artifact of one device. Empirically it does not bite, $1/Z_r$ staying below
$1.33$ at the tuned $\eta$ over $d\in\{10,50,200\}$, which is why
Conjecture~\ref{conj:add} is stated rather than abandoned.

\section{Stationary sensors}\label{app:sensor}

\begin{observation}\label{obs:sensor}
The multiplicative term $\sqrt{\min\{|\Sset|,d\}T}$ is not forced by the known construction, and
is absent at one endpoint of the present model class.
\end{observation}

The $\Omega(\sqrt{\min\{|\Sset|,d\}T})$ construction of \citet[Thm.~5.2]{esposito2023} re-splits
states across actions adversarially per block, so its action-to-state map is time-varying and
lies outside the model class of Section~\ref{sec:setting}. At $\etwo=0$ with stationary $P$ the
problem is a stochastic delayed bandit, where delay enters additively \citep{joulani2013}. One
route to it is therefore unavailable and the term is absent at one endpoint, but neither a
matching upper bound for all $\etwo>0$ nor a lower bound ruling it out is established here.
